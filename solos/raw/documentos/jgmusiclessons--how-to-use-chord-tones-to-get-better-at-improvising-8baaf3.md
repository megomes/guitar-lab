# How to Confidently Solo Over Chord Progressions with Chord Tones On Guitar

Fonte: https://jgmusiclessons.com/how-to-use-chord-tones-to-get-better-at-improvising/
Using chord tones helps your improvised lines connect more clearly to the harmony underneath them. Instead of only thinking about one scale over an entire progression, you can aim for specific notes as the chords change.

Chord tones are the notes that make up a chord, such as the root, 3rd, 5th, and 7th. In this lesson, we’ll practice finding those notes, targeting them through a progression, and connecting them into melodic phrases.

Start by targeting the root or 3rd of each chord in a short progression. Once you can follow the chord changes, connect those target notes with a few nearby scale notes to create a simple melodic line.

## Chord Tones Explained

Chord tones are the notes that make up a chord. The root identifies the chord, while notes such as the 3rd, 5th, and 7th help define its quality and sound.

These notes can also be described by their interval or scale-degree relationship to the root.

### Intervals and Scale-Degree Labels

This chart shows common interval names and the scale-degree labels used to describe notes in relation to a root. Which of these notes function as chord tones depends on the chord you’re playing.

| Interval | Chord Tone | 
|---|---|
| Unison | Root | 
| minor 2nd | flat 2 or b2 | 
| Major 2nd | 2 | 
| minor 3rd | flat 3rd or b3 | 
| Major 3rd | 3 | 
| Perfect 4th | 4 | 
| Augmented 4th | sharp 4 or #4 | 
| Perfect 5th | 5 |

| Major 13th | 13 | 

### Chord Tones in Triad Chords

Start with triads because they give you the three basic chord tones: root, 3rd, and 5th.

- Major chord – 1, 3, and 5. For example, C Major has the chord tones C, E, and G.
- Minor chord – 1, b3, and 5. For example, C minor has the chord tones C, Eb, and G.
- Diminished chord – 1, b3, and b5. For example, C diminished has the chord tones C, Eb, and Gb.

### Chord Tones in 7th chords

7th chords add one more chord tone, giving you another note you can emphasize when improvising.

- Major 7 chord – 1, 3, 5, and 7. For example, C Major 7 has the chord tones C, E, G, and B.
- Minor 7 chord – 1, b3, 5, and b7. For example, C minor 7 has the chord tones C, Eb, G, and Bb.
- Dominant 7 chord – 1, 3, 5, and b7. For example, C 7 has the chord tones C, E, G, and Bb.
- Minor 7 b5 chord (aka half diminished) – 1, b3, b5, and b7. For example, C half diminished has the chord tones C, Eb, Gb, and Bb.
- Diminished 7 chord – 1, b3, b5, bb7 (same as Major 6). For example, C diminished 7 has the chord tones C, Eb, Gb, and A.

## Targeting Chord Tones

The first step is learning to follow the chord changes with one specific note from each chord.

Start with roots because they are easiest to identify. Then repeat the exercise with 3rds, which help you hear the difference between Major and minor chord qualities more clearly.

### Targeting Root Notes

### Targeting 3rds

Try this: Use a short progression of two to four chords. First play one root note as each chord changes. Then repeat the progression and target the 3rd of each chord instead. Keep your place through the progression even if you need a moment to find the next note.

Once roots and 3rds feel clearer, use the 5th and 7th examples to expand the same exercise with different chord tones.

Keep in mind, your actual solos won’t sound exactly like these exercises. Instead, think of them as a tool to sharpen your awareness of where the key notes are located when chords change.

## Using Chord Tones to Create Smooth Voice Leading

Once you can find chord tones, look for nearby notes that connect one chord to the next. Moving to a close chord tone, or keeping a note that two chords share, can make the change sound smoother.

This is one way to think about voice leading: instead of jumping to a completely new area of the fretboard for every chord, look for the closest useful note.

For example, here are some common notes between the following chords:

- D minor 7 and G 7 share the chord tones D and F.
- G 7 and C Major 7 share the chord tones G and B.
- C Major 7 and the A 7 share the chord tones E, and G.

## Using Approach Notes

Once you can hear and locate your target chord tone, you can create more movement by approaching it from a nearby note.

- **Chromatic approach:** move into the target from a note one fret above or below it.
- **Diatonic approach:** move into the target from a nearby note in the related scale.

**Example:**

- Target note: G
- Approach: F# (below chromatic), A (above diatonic)

You can also use two notes before targeting a note, whether from below, above or both.

This technique creates a sense of build-up and release, which makes your soloing sound more musical and expressive.

## Think Like a Composer

Chord tones give you destinations, but you still decide how the phrase gets there. Use the same phrasing ideas from the previous lesson to turn these target notes into music.

- **Develop motifs** (repeat and vary ideas)
- **Leave space** (don’t play nonstop)
- **Use dynamics and phrasing**
- **Experiment with rhythm** (i.e. don’t always start on beat 1)
- **Use scale patterns to generate ideas**

## Writing Out Chord Tones

Writing out chord tones can help you see the progression before trying to improvise over it. Write each chord, list its chord tones, then look for notes that are shared or sit close together between the changes.

Afterward, take those ideas to the guitar and see where the notes sit on the fretboard.

You can use something like the **My Music Composition Notebook** (with printable sheet music and guides), or a free tool like Musescore to input notes digitally.

One of my music teachers suggested this exercise, and I used to keep a notebook filled with chord tone and scale ideas. Mapping out lines for different progressions really helped me connect the theory to my playing.

You can do this even when you’re away from your instrument.

## Wrapping Up

Chord tones help your improvised phrases follow the harmony instead of floating over the progression without reacting to the chord changes.

Start simply. Follow the progression using roots, then try 3rds. Once you can hear and find those targets, connect them with nearby scale notes to create short melodic phrases.

From there, arpeggios, voice leading, extensions, and approach notes give you more ways to expand the same basic idea: know where the chord is going and give your phrase a clear note to aim for.

**Before continuing**

You’re ready to continue when you can follow a short chord progression by targeting the root or 3rd of each chord, then connect those target notes with a simple melodic phrase without losing your place in the progression.
